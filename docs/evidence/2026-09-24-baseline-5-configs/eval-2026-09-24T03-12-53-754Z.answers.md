While supply and demand in microeconomics suggest that prices are set where the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded, behavioral economics introduces psychological factors that can cause individuals to deviate from rational behavior, leading to price anomalies. Traditional models assume market participants are fully rational, but behavioral economics shows that biases, heuristics, and framing can lead to irrational decisions, causing prices to differ from what supply and demand would predict.

In supply and demand, factors like scarcity, consumer preferences, and production costs directly influence price changes. However, behavioral economics highlights that individuals might overvalue immediate rewards or undervalue future consequences, leading to herd behavior, anchoring to initial prices, or other irrational patterns not accounted for by simple supply and demand.

Furthermore, behavioral economics suggests that market participants are not always utility-maximizers; they can be influenced by emotions and cognitive biases, resulting in phenomena such as price stickiness, where prices do not adjust quickly to changes in supply and demand due to factors like status quo bias or loss aversion.

In essence, while supply and demand provide a foundational framework for understanding price mechanisms, behavioral economics reveals a more nuanced picture where human psychology can cause systematic deviations from the predictions of this model.

The immune system responds to pathogens through a series of steps. Initially, innate immunity provides a general defense, using barriers like skin and certain immune cells to detect and respond to pathogens. If the pathogen persists, the adaptive immune system kicks in, with B-cells and T-cells specifically recognizing and attacking the invader. B-cells produce antibodies that bind to antigens on the pathogen's surface. The complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of these antibodies are crucial for their specificity and binding to the antigen.

Vaccines work by mimicking this natural process. They introduce an agent that resembles the pathogen, but is typically inactivated, weakened, or a part of it. For example, a vaccine could contain a harmless form of the virus or a protein from the virus. When vaccinated, the immune system is exposed to the antigen, and B-cells create antibodies, including those with specific CDRs, against it. Memory B-cells are also generated, ensuring a quicker and more effective response if the real pathogen is encountered in the future
