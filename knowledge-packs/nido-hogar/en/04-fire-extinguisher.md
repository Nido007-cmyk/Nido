An extinguisher is for a small fire that has just started, not for a fire that is already established. Knowing when NOT to use one saves more lives than knowing how. Your local fire department can teach you and let you practice.

## First: the decision (every answer must be "yes")
Use an extinguisher **only if you can answer yes to all of these**:
1. Have I alerted others in the building that there is a fire?
2. Has someone called (or is someone calling) the fire department?
3. Am I physically able to use it? Young children and older adults should not use fire extinguishers.
4. Is the fire small and contained in a single object or surface, like a pan or a wastebasket?
5. Am I safe from the fire's toxic smoke?
6. Do I have a clear escape route?
If any answer is "no", or you are unsure: alert others, leave the building and call your emergency number from a mobile or a neighbor's phone.

## How to use it: the PASS word
Remember the word **PASS**:
1. **P — Pull the pin.** Hold the extinguisher with the nozzle pointing away from you and release the locking mechanism.
2. **A — Aim low.** Point the extinguisher at the **base** of the fire, not at the flames.
3. **S — Squeeze the lever** slowly and evenly.
4. **S — Sweep the nozzle** from side to side, across the base, until the fire is out.
If the fire does not go out, starts growing or the room fills with smoke: drop the attempt, get out and close the door behind you.

## Basic types (the letters on the label)
- **A:** ordinary materials: cloth, wood and paper.
- **B:** flammable liquids: grease, gasoline, oil and oil-based paints.
- **C:** plugged-in electrical equipment (the agent does not conduct electricity).
- **D:** flammable metals (mostly found in factories).
- **K:** vegetable oils, animal oils and fats in cooking appliances (mostly commercial kitchens).
For the home, a **multipurpose ABC** (or B-C) extinguisher covers most home fires. Look for the "UL Listed" or "ULC Listed" label from a recognized testing laboratory, and choose the largest extinguisher you can safely and comfortably operate. Read the instructions **before** you need it, and store it near an exit, with nothing blocking access to it.

## When never to try
- The fire has spread beyond the first thing that caught, or has reached walls or the ceiling.
- The room is filling with smoke or you cannot see your way out.
- You do not know what is burning, or it is a gas fire that is still leaking.
- The extinguisher runs out and the fire is still going.
In all of those cases: get out, close the door behind you and call from outside.

## Keep it ready
Check from time to time that it is easy to reach, that the pressure gauge shows the recommended level, and that the can, hoses and nozzles are not damaged, dented or rusted, and keep dust, oil and grease off the outside. Some extinguishers need to be shaken monthly and others need a pressure test every few years: follow the manufacturer's guidelines; if the pressure is too low, have it recharged or replaced as the model requires.
