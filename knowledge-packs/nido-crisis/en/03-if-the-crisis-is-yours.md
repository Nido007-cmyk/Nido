If you are reading this at a very bad moment: you don't have to solve your whole life today. The goal for the next few hours is smaller and possible: to get to tomorrow with company and with less risk. The most intense urges rise and fall like a wave; many people find the worst moment passes if they gain time and are not alone.

## Right now, in this order
1. **Move away from anything you could use to harm yourself.** Leave that room, hand over or put away those objects, or go somewhere with people. Putting time and distance between the urge and the means saves lives.
2. **Don't be alone.** Call or message a person you trust and tell the truth, even in few words: "I'm in a really bad place and I'm afraid I might hurt myself. Can you stay with me or talk for a while?"
3. **Call your country's crisis line.** They answer every day, at any hour, free of charge and without judging; you can also call just to talk, without waiting until you feel "worse". If you don't know the number, it is in this pack's verified facts table.
4. **Look after the basics while the wave passes:** breathe out slowly, drink water, keep warm or take a shower, switch off the news and social media for a while. Avoid alcohol and other drugs: they loosen your brakes exactly when you need them most.
5. **Postpone any decision.** Give yourself a concrete, short deadline — tonight, until tomorrow — and keep it as an agreement with yourself. There will be time to think about problems one at a time.

## Your personal safety plan
A safety plan is a short sheet, written at a calmer moment (you can make it with a professional, with the crisis line, or with someone you trust), that tells you what to do when the crisis comes back. The SAMHSA model, adapted from Stanley and Brown, has 6 steps, in order:

1. **My warning signs:** thoughts, images, moods or behaviours that warn me a crisis is coming (for example: stopping replying to messages, not sleeping, thinking I'm a burden).
2. **What I can do on my own to distract or calm myself:** walking, music, a shower, exercise, slow breathing, going somewhere with people.
3. **People and places that distract me without having to explain anything:** a friend to talk about something else with, a park, a café.
4. **People I can actually ask for help and tell how I am:** names and phone numbers, written down.
5. **Professionals and lines I can call:** my therapist or doctor, my country's crisis line, the emergency department.
6. **How I make my environment safer:** what I will put away, lock up or hand to someone while the risk lasts.

Keep it where you will find it in a bad moment (on your phone, on paper in your wallet) and share it with the people in your step 4. Review it if something stops working. It is not a contract or a promise: it is a tool, and using any step — or jumping straight to calling — already means it is working.

## Get urgent help, without waiting, if…
- You have already harmed yourself or have started acting to do so.
- You have a concrete plan, a time in mind and access to the means, and you feel you may not be able to stop yourself.
- You cannot keep yourself safe or stay with someone.
In that case call your country's emergency number or go to an emergency department now, and tell someone. Asking for urgent help is not overreacting: it is exactly what it is for.

## And tomorrow
When things are calmer, find the next support: an appointment with a professional, telling your family or a friend, reviewing your plan. If you are already in treatment, tell your therapist even if you feel ashamed. Getting through a crisis does not mean you are "cured", and it doesn't mean you failed either: it means you need more support, and it exists.
