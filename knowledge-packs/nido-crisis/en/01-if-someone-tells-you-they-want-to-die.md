Being told by someone that they want to die is frightening, and it is normal not to know what to say. You don't need to be a professional or find perfect words: staying, listening and helping them connect with help is already a lot. Talking about suicide does not put the idea in their head; it usually brings relief and opens the door to asking for help.

## Signs to take seriously
- They talk about wanting to die, about having no reason to live, or about being a burden to others.
- They say they feel trapped or in unbearable pain.
- They look for ways to harm themselves, or say goodbye as if they won't be back.
- They withdraw, stop doing what they used to do, sleep far too much or hardly at all.
- They drink more alcohol or use more drugs, act with rage, or become suddenly calm after being very unwell.
One sign does not always mean there is a plan, but every sign deserves a conversation, not waiting to see if it passes.

## What to do, step by step
1. **Find a quiet time and place.** No rushing, no screens in between.
2. **Ask directly and calmly:** "I'm worried about you. Have you been thinking about taking your own life?" Asking this way does not increase the risk; it gives them permission to tell you the truth.
3. **Listen without judging.** Let them talk, don't interrupt, don't try to fix everything. Say back what you understand: "It sounds like you feel you can't go on."
4. **Don't leave them alone if the risk is immediate.** If they say they are going to do it now, or have already harmed themselves, call your country's emergency number or go with them to an emergency department. If you can, move away anything they could use to harm themselves.
5. **Help them connect with help today, not "someday".** Offer to call your country's crisis line together, to tell a trusted family member, or to go with them to an appointment. Offering to go with them helps more than handing them a number and leaving.
6. **Ask again in the following days.** A short message ("how are you doing today?") keeps the bridge open; the crisis doesn't always end when the conversation does.

## What to say
- "Thank you for telling me. It matters to me that you're here."
- "You don't have to go through this alone. Let's find help together."
- "I don't know exactly what to say, but I'm staying with you."
- "What would help you right now?"

## What not to say
- "Don't talk nonsense" or "other people have it worse": this dismisses their pain and shuts the conversation down.
- "Think of your family" used as a reproach: guilt does not protect, it overwhelms.
- "Promise me you won't do it" as the only measure: a promise is no substitute for help. Better a plan with steps and people, like the safety plan guide in this pack.
- Don't promise to keep it secret if their life is at risk. You can say: "This is too important to keep to myself; I'm going to get help, and I'll tell you before I do."

## If you're not sure it's "that serious"
Treat it as if it were. An awkward conversation is better than a missed chance. And if you are left shaken after helping, seek support yourself: crisis lines also take calls from family and friends who are worried about someone else.
