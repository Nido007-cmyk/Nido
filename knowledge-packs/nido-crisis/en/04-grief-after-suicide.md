Losing someone to suicide is a different kind of grief: alongside the pain there is often guilt ("could I have prevented it?"), unanswered questions, anger, shame, or a guilty sense of relief if the person had been suffering greatly. All of that is common in this grief, and it does not mean you are doing it wrong or that you loved them any less. Suicide has many causes that come together; it is not the fault of one person, or of one conversation that didn't happen.

## If you are supporting someone who has lost a person this way
- **Say something, even something simple.** Other people's silence is one of the things that hurts most. This is enough: "I heard about your brother. I'm so sorry. I'm here." Use the name of the person who died: their family usually finds comfort in hearing their name and talking about their life, not only about their death.
- **Listen without trying to fix it.** Let them repeat the same things many times, cry or not cry. Don't search for the perfect phrase; your steady presence matters more.
- **Don't say:** "they were a coward" or "they were selfish", "you should be over it by now", "I know how you feel" (unless you have been through the same), and don't ask for details of how they died. Avoid giving a single cause ("it was the breakup", "it was the job"): nobody knows the whole of it.
- **Practical, concrete help:** bringing food, going with them to an appointment or an office, looking after the children for an afternoon, picking someone up from the airport. Offer something specific ("I'll bring your shopping on Thursday") instead of a vague "call me if you need anything", because grieving people find it very hard to ask.
- **Stay around afterwards, too.** At first there are many people; weeks later, when almost nobody asks any more, the grief is still there. Remember important dates (birthdays, the anniversary) and keep inviting them, even if they often say no.
- **Watch over them with care.** People bereaved by suicide are at higher risk of depression and of having suicidal thoughts themselves. If you see they cannot cope with daily life after some time, withdraw completely, or talk about wanting to die, help them find professional support or a crisis line, just as you would for anyone else at risk.

## If you are the one who has lost them

- **There is no timetable.** There will be bearable days and days that break open months later, sometimes because of a song or a smell. That is not going backwards: it is the shape of this grief.
- **Talk about it with people who can listen,** and if you can, find a group of people who have lost someone the same way: being understood without having to explain helps a great deal. Ask your country's crisis line or health services about suicide bereavement groups.
- **Look after the basics, even at minimum level:** sleep, eating something simple, getting out into daylight, small routines. Grief exhausts the body.
- **With children,** tell the truth in simple words suited to their age, without harsh details and without inventing another cause: that the person died because their mind was very ill and stopped working frightens children less than silence and secrets, which children fill with blame of their own.
- **Seek professional help** if the pain still doesn't let you breathe after months, if you cannot work or care for your people, or if thoughts of harming yourself appear: then also use the "If the crisis is yours" guide in this pack and your country's crisis line.
