After a major disaster, organised help can take 72 or more hours to reach your neighbourhood. In that time, your home and your street run on what you had stored and on what you organise among neighbours. The family plan and go-bag guides in the Essentials pack say what to have; this guide says how to organise those days.

## The first hours
1. Check that you and yours are all right and treat injuries. Remember the hazard may return: aftershocks after an earthquake, further flooding after a flood, flare-ups after a fire.
2. Let people know you are safe with **one text message** to your out-of-area contact: texts may be more reliable than phone calls and they save battery power. Use the phone only for emergencies: responders need the lines to coordinate their work.
3. Listen to a battery-operated or hand-crank radio for official information. Do not spread rumours and do not act on them (CENAPRED): check what reaches you from neighbours or social media against the radio and official sources.
4. If your home is damaged, follow the returning-home guide before you treat any part of it as safe.

## Water and food: order from day one
1. Take inventory on the first day: how much drinking water and food you have, and for how many people. The home-supply benchmark is one gallon — about 4 litres — of water per person per day, for drinking and sanitation (Ready.gov).
2. Drink and give others what they need: rationing is not going thirsty, it is not wasting. Keep drinking water for drinking and cooking, and use non-drinking water (rainwater) for flushing or cleaning.
3. Eat the fridge food first, then the freezer, then cans and dry food: that way you use what would spoil first. The food-in-emergencies guide in the Essentials pack gives the safe times without refrigeration.
4. If tap water is not safe, boil it or make it safe before drinking, as the drinking-water guide in the Essentials pack explains. Do not eat or drink anything that has been in contact with broken glass, rubble, dust or any contaminant (CENAPRED).

## Information and communication with no network
- Set radio listening times (for example, on the hour) and turn it off in between to save batteries.
- Leave a note on your door saying where you are and how to reach you if you go out: whoever comes looking won't spend your battery or theirs.
- To reach family, repeat the short message by text, or by NIDO between paired phones with no internet and no coverage, instead of long calls.

## The neighbourhood: you are each other's first help
1. **Check on others.** If you are OK, now is the time to check on others: help can sometimes be 72 or more hours away, and a neighbour may need help getting water, food or blankets (Red Cross).
2. Make a list for your street: who lives alone, who is older, has a disability or an illness, who has babies, and who has tools, a generator, a car with fuel or first aid training.
3. Help safely: disasters can make normally safe places structurally unsound. Do not enter damaged buildings to help, and do not try to remove heavy debris by yourself.
4. Organise what is shared: a meeting and information point on the street, turns to look after children and dependent people, and shared supplies if one home is short of water or food and another has spare.
5. To help beyond your street, the most useful thing is a financial donation to a recognised organisation already working there: money buys exactly what is needed, while donated goods have to be sorted, cleaned, stored and shipped.

## Days 2 and 3: routine and official help
- Build a routine: meals at set times, rest, care for the children, and time away from disaster news. Returning to as many of your personal and family routines as possible helps emotionally too.
- Find your municipality's official aid points (water and food distribution, shelters, phone-charging and information points) and share their location with your street.
- Write down on paper what matters: damage, expenses, medications running out, and each home's needs.
- Look after sanitation from the first day (see the emergency sanitation guide in the Essentials pack).

## Important
Based on the Red Cross, Ready.gov (FEMA) and CENAPRED. The 72 hours are a reference, not a promise: with two weeks of home supplies, the same organisation works even if help takes longer.
