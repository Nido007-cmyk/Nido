Almost all children bump their heads now and then, and most head injuries are minor. The head injury guide in the Essentials pack also covers the spine; this guide focuses on deciding, by age and by the fall, whether watching at home is enough or waiting is not an option. Note: the AAP article behind this guide is not intended for children younger than 2 years, or for possible neck injuries: for them, the bar for getting checked is lower.

## Right after the fall
- For anything more than a light bump on the head, call your child's doctor: they will want to know when and how the injury happened and how your child is feeling.
- If your child is alert and responds to you, the head injury is usually mild and no tests or X-rays are needed. They may cry from pain or fright, but this should last no longer than 10 minutes.
- Apply a cold compress for 20 minutes to help the swelling go down.
- If the head is bleeding, apply pressure for 5 to 10 minutes; if it keeps bleeding, they may need to be seen at an urgent treatment centre.

## Watching at home: the first 24 hours
Only if the bump was light, they are their usual self, and a professional has not said otherwise:
- You or another responsible adult should stay with your child for the first 24 hours, ready to take them back to the doctor or hospital if there is a problem. Signs of a more serious injury can also appear within 48 hours, and in a few cases take days.
- It is okay for your child to go to sleep. If their doctor has checked them and allows it, they may recommend checking every 2 to 3 hours that they can move normally, wake enough to recognize you, and respond to you.
- For pain, give only acetaminophen (paracetamol) unless your child's doctor says otherwise.
- Avoid rough play and contact sports while they recover; a repeat head injury during recovery can cause long-term damage to a child's brain.

## High-impact falls: get checked promptly even if they seem fine
- A fall from a height of more than 1 metre (more than the child's own height, or more than 5 stairs).
- A road traffic accident.
- A bruise, swelling or cut of more than 5 cm on the head.
- Any head bump in a baby under 1 year with a visible bump: have a professional check it without waiting.

## Do not wait: emergency care if any of these appear
- They lost consciousness, even briefly. If they cannot be woken, have convulsions or have difficulty breathing, call your emergency number.
- Vomiting more than 2 or 3 times.
- A constant headache, particularly one that gets worse and does not go away with pain relief.
- Slurred speech, confusion, or difficulty recognizing familiar people.
- Dizziness that does not go away or happens repeatedly, stumbling or difficulty walking.
- Extreme irritability or other abnormal behavior; in a baby, crying that will not stop and cannot be consoled, or refusing to nurse or eat.
- Oozing blood or watery fluid from the nose or ears.
- Difficulty waking up or excessive sleepiness.
- Unequal size of the pupils (the dark centre part of the eyes), double or blurry vision.
- Unusual paleness that lasts for more than an hour.
- Convulsions, weakness of arms or legs, or persistent ringing in the ears.

## Concussion without being knocked out
A concussion can happen even without losing consciousness: mild headache, feeling sick without vomiting, dizziness, bad temper, problems concentrating or remembering, tiredness, lack of appetite or problems sleeping. It can last for days or weeks. The return to normal activities and school should be gradual.

## Important
