The fever guide in the Essentials pack gives the general rules. This guide adds what changes with age, because in children the same temperature means very different things at 2 months and at 4 years. A fever is 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher. In babies, a rectal reading is the most reliable, especially under 3 months; do not use an ear thermometer under 6 months, because their ear canals are too small for an accurate reading.

## Under 3 months: always urgent
If your baby is 3 months old or younger and has a rectal temperature of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher, call your pediatrician or go to urgent care right away, even if they look well and have no other symptoms. Do not wait to see how it goes.

Why the hurry? A baby this young has an immature immune system and cannot yet show the signs of a serious infection clearly: a serious blood infection (sepsis) or infection of the membranes around the brain (meningitis) can begin with fever alone. The doctor needs to examine the baby to rule out any serious infection or disease. For the same reason, do not give fever medicine to a baby under 3 months without talking to the pediatrician first.

## 3 to 6 months
Get urgent advice the same day if the temperature reaches 39 °C or higher. The American Academy of Pediatrics also says to notify the doctor at 101 °F (38.3 °C) or greater in this age group, because such a temperature may point to a significant infection or dehydration, which may need treatment. At any age, how the child seems matters more than the number alone: if they are very drowsy, will not eat, breathe poorly or worry you, do not wait for it to climb higher.

## Over 6 months and older children
Notify the doctor if the temperature reaches 103 °F (39.4 °C) or higher. In an older child who is eating, sleeping and has playful moments, fever alone is rarely an emergency: decide by the symptoms that come with it (severe earache or sore throat, a bad cough, a rash, repeated vomiting or diarrhea, unusual fussiness, or sleeping much more than usual).

## How long it lasts, and what is normal
The temperature usually returns to normal within 1 to 4 days. Get medical advice if a high temperature has lasted for 5 days or more. Teething can cause a slight rise in temperature within the normal range, but research shows it does not cause a true fever: if the reading is 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher, your baby is probably sick from something else.

## Care at home (when the age allows it)
- Plenty of fluids; if your baby is breastfed, continue to breastfeed as normal.
- Check on your child regularly, including during the night, and watch for signs of dehydration (nappies that are not very wet, sunken eyes, no tears when crying).
- Give paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen only if they are distressed or uncomfortable, at the dose on the packaging for their age and weight. Do not give paracetamol to a child under 2 months. Do not give ibuprofen to a child under 3 months, weighing under 5 kg, or who has chickenpox or is dehydrated. Never give aspirin to children under 16.
- Do not undress your child or sponge them down to cool them, and do not wrap them in too many clothes or bedclothes: a high temperature is a natural and healthy response to infection.

## Go to emergency care now, at any age, if...
- They have a stiff neck, are bothered by light, or have a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it.
- They have a fit (febrile seizure) for the first time.
- They have unusually cold hands and feet, or blue, grey, paler than usual or blotchy skin, lips or tongue.
- They are drowsy and hard to wake, will not stop crying, or are confused.
- They have difficulty breathing, are breathless or breathing very fast, or make grunting noises.
- They are not responding like they normally do, or are not interested in feeding or normal activities.
