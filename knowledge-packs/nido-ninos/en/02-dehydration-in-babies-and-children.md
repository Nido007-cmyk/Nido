Babies and young children dehydrate much faster than an adult: they lose fluid with diarrhea, vomiting or fever and cannot ask for water. The oral rehydration recipe and the general amounts are in the diarrhea and oral rehydration guide in the Essentials pack; this guide teaches you to spot dehydration by age, which is what decides how urgent it is.

## Signs in a baby (under 1 year)
- Far fewer wet nappies than usual.
- Few or no tears when they cry.
- A sunken fontanelle: the soft spot on top of their head sinks inwards.
- Sunken eyes.
- A dry mouth.
- Being drowsy or irritable, not their usual self.

The fontanelle and the nappies are your two home gauges: a baby cannot say "I'm thirsty".

## Signs in an older child
- Peeing less often than usual, with dark yellow, strong-smelling pee.
- A dry mouth, lips and tongue.
- Sunken eyes.
- Feeling tired, dizzy or lightheaded.
- Feeling very thirsty.

## What to do at home while there are no serious signs
- Carry on breastfeeding or using formula, in small amounts more often than usual. Do not make formula weaker: prepare it as normal.
- Give oral rehydration solution in regular small sips. After each loose stool: 50 to 100 ml for children under 2 years, and 100 to 200 ml for children 2 to 10 years. If they vomit, wait 10 minutes and continue more slowly.
- Give your baby small sips of extra water if they are on formula or solid foods (if your baby is under 6 months, boil the water first and let it cool).
- Give small children their usual diet. Do not give young children fruit juice or fizzy drinks: they can make diarrhea or vomiting worse.

## Get urgent advice the same day if...
- Your baby has fewer wet nappies, or your child is peeing much less than normal and it is dark yellow.
- Your baby or child has few or no tears when they cry.
- Your baby's fontanelle is sunken.
- Your child seems unusually drowsy, or feels dizzy when standing and it does not go away.
- They are breathing quickly or have a fast heart rate.
- They cannot drink, or vomit everything they take.

## Go to emergency care now if...
- They have blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue (on brown or black skin, this may be easier to see on the palms of the hands or soles of the feet).
- Their skin feels cold.
- They have difficulty breathing, or are taking lots of quick breaths.
- They are confused.
- They are sleepier than normal or difficult to wake.

These can be signs of shock, the serious complication of dehydration.

## Important
Based on the NHS and on WHO and UNICEF (IMCI). A baby with signs of dehydration does not get better by "sleeping it off": have a professional see them today.
