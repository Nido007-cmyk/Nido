If someone collapses, does not respond and is not breathing normally (or is only gasping), their heart has probably stopped. Every minute without CPR sharply lowers the chance of survival. Starting quickly matters most: do not be afraid of doing it "wrong"; doing nothing is worse.

## Before you start
- Check that the scene is safe.
- Tap their shoulders and speak loudly. If they do not respond, check whether they breathe normally (no more than 10 seconds).
- Ask someone to call the emergency number and bring a defibrillator (AED). If you are alone, call on speakerphone and start.

## Chest compressions
1. Lay the person on their back on a firm surface (the floor).
2. Kneel beside them. Put the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half of the breastbone) and the other hand on top.
3. Arms straight, shoulders right above your hands. Push down using your body weight.
4. Depth: about 5 cm (2 inches), no more than 6 cm, in adults.
5. Rate: 100 to 120 per minute (the beat of "Stayin' Alive").
6. Let the chest come all the way back up between compressions, without lifting your hands.
7. Keep pauses as short as possible.

## Rescue breaths (only if you know how)
- If you are trained and willing: 30 compressions and 2 breaths, and repeat.
- To give a breath: tilt the head back and lift the chin, pinch the nose, seal your mouth over theirs and blow for 1 second until you see the chest rise.
- If you don't know how or don't want to, do continuous compressions only: in adults this works well.

## When to stop
- When a defibrillator arrives (use it at once, see the AED guide).
- When the person starts breathing normally or moves.
- When emergency services arrive and take over.
- If you are exhausted or the scene becomes dangerous. If someone else can help, switch every 2 minutes.

## Opioid overdose
If you suspect an overdose (pinpoint pupils, blue lips, opioid drugs or medicines nearby) and you have naloxone: call the emergency number, start CPR and give the naloxone following its instructions.

## Important
Based on the AHA 2025 guidelines and the Red Cross. Learn CPR in a hands-on course: that is how you do it right when it matters. Always call your emergency number.
