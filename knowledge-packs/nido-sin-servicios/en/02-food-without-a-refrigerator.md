Without cold, food safety comes down to two things: time and temperature. The NIDO Essentials emergency food guide gives the general refrigerator and freezer rules; this one goes food by food.

## The time and temperature rule
- Bacteria multiply quickly between **40°F and 140°F (4°C and 60°C)**. Holding food below 5°C or above 60°C slows or stops their growth (WHO).
- **Do not leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours.**
- Throw out any perishable food that has been **above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours**.
- **Never taste** food to check whether it is safe: some dangerous bacteria, and the toxin that causes botulism, do not change how food tastes, smells or looks. When in doubt, throw it out.

## What keeps in the pantry (unopened)
These are "shelf-stable" foods: they can be stored at room temperature until opened. Watch out: some canned products are labeled "Keep Refrigerated" (certain canned hams and seafood) — those are **not** pantry foods.

USDA times for unopened containers stored in a cool, dry place:
- Low-acid canned goods (meat, beans, vegetables, soups): **2 to 5 years**.
- High-acid canned goods (tomatoes, fruit, juices, pickles): **12 to 18 months**.
- Home-canned foods: **12 months**.
- Rice and dried pasta: **2 years**.
- Tuna and other seafood in retort pouches: **18 months**.
- Commercially packaged jerky: **12 months**.

How to store them:
- In a cool, dry place, **below 85°F (29°C)**. Never above or beside the stove, under the sink, or in a damp garage or basement.
- Check your pantry every few weeks and use the oldest items first.

## Once opened, it is no longer a pantry food
Once a can is opened, bacteria can get in: any unused portion becomes perishable food and would need refrigeration. Without a refrigerator, **open only what will be eaten at that meal**.

## From the refrigerator, after more than 2 hours above 40°F (4°C)
From the FoodSafety.gov (USDA) chart:

**You can keep:**
- Hard cheeses (such as Cheddar, Swiss, Parmesan) and processed cheeses.
- Butter and margarine.
- Whole, uncut fresh fruit and vegetables.
- Bread, rolls, tortillas and fruit pies.
- Peanut butter, jelly, mustard, catsup, olives and pickles.
- Opened fruit juices and opened canned fruit.

**Throw it out, without hesitation:**
- Meat, poultry, fish and seafood, raw or cooked, and opened canned meat or fish.
- Milk, cream, yogurt and soft cheeses (cottage, cream, mozzarella, ricotta, Brie), shredded cheeses and low-fat cheeses.
- Eggs and egg dishes.
- All leftovers: casseroles, soups, pizza, cooked rice and pasta, cooked potatoes.
- Cut fruit and vegetables, prepared salads and opened creamy dressings.

## Botulism: the danger you cannot see
The toxin that causes botulism **cannot be seen, smelled or tasted**: taking even a small taste of contaminated food can be deadly. The greatest risk is in improperly home-canned foods and in damaged containers.

Throw the whole container out, without tasting it, if:
- It is leaking, bulging or swollen, or heavily rusted.
- It is badly dented, especially on a seam, or a jar is cracked or has a loose or bulging lid.
- It spurts liquid or foam when opened, or the food is discolored, moldy or smells bad.

To throw a suspect container away safely: put it in a tightly closed plastic bag, inside another bag, and into a non-recyclable trash container outside the home, where children and animals cannot get at it.

As an extra precaution, boil home-canned low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, tomatoes and foods containing them) before eating: **10 minutes** at altitudes below 1,000 feet, adding 1 minute for each additional 1,000 feet. This never rescues a container with signs of contamination: that one is always thrown out.
